# The Three Languages Hypothesis: Natural Language, Mathematics, and Art as Epistemic Registers

**Manuscript type:** Critical conceptual analysis and proposed research programme  
**Status:** Working manuscript; not peer reviewed  
**Date:** 22 November 2026  
**Authorship and affiliations:** To be confirmed before submission

## Abstract

The three languages hypothesis, articulated by Oliveira Jr. and Oliveira, proposes that human knowledge is fundamentally created and transmitted through natural languages, mathematical formalisms, and the arts. Its stronger version suggests that mastery of these languages could suffice for any human activity. We analyze this proposal against philosophy of symbolic forms, semiotics, cognitive theories of multiple representation, aesthetics, symbol grounding, diagrammatic reasoning, and automated science. This literature supports the importance of linguistic, formal, and artistic practices, but neither establishes that the three exhaust knowledge nor that expressive fluency entails practical competence. The categories are also heterogeneous: natural language and mathematics designate relatively constrained symbolic practices, while the arts include diverse, overlapping practices that need not share a syntax. We propose a reconstruction as three **epistemic registers**: discursive, formal-relational, and aesthetic-experiential. The registers are task-relative, partly overlapping orientations of representation, not mutually exclusive media, cognitive modules, or fundamental natural kinds. We distinguish descriptive prominence, complementarity, completeness, and sufficiency as logically separate hypotheses; specify criteria for translations between registers; and propose comparisons against two-register, alternative three-register, four-register, and multidimensional models. The resulting framework offers a philosophical vocabulary for plural kinds of understanding and a technological research programme for auditable AI transformations among prose, formal models, and expressive artifacts. Sensory stimulation is a peripheral illustration rather than a central premise.

**Keywords:** three languages hypothesis; symbolic forms; epistemology; natural language; mathematics; arts; semiotics; cognitive science; artificial intelligence; multimodal representation

## 1. Introduction

Oliveira Jr. and Oliveira [1] propose three broad “languages” through which knowledge is produced and communicated: natural languages, the language of mathematical formalisms, and the language of the arts. In their account, mastery of all three by an intelligent machine could contribute to its capacity to exceed human intellectual performance. The inclusion of the arts is a distinctive and promising feature of the proposal. It resists the common assumption that knowledge is exclusively propositional or numerical.

But the argument contains several claims that require separate examination. **Prominence:** are these major forms in actual intellectual practice? **Complementarity:** do they enable different inferences or kinds of understanding? **Completeness:** can all human knowledge be assigned to one or more of them without stretching their definitions? **Sufficiency:** would mastery of them enable all human activities? Evidence for one claim does not establish the others.

We argue for a bounded reconstruction. Discursive, formal-relational, and aesthetic-experiential practices are three useful but overlapping **epistemic registers**. They organize different affordances for expression, reasoning, discovery, interpretation, and communication. Their significance is conditional on the task and on what is being evaluated. The choice of *three* must earn its place by predicting something beyond rival taxonomies.

The present article is a critical conceptual synthesis, not a systematic literature review or empirical demonstration. It develops explicit definitions, encounters historical precedents and counterarguments, identifies testable consequences, and separates the philosophical and technological value of the proposal from unproven claims of universal intelligence. It does not postulate three distinct brain systems.

## 2. Defining the three languages

### 2.1. Languages, media, practices, and knowledge

A **carrier** can be sound, text, image, motion, or a physical object. A **representational system** specifies ways to refer, distinguish, combine, or transform expressions. A **practice** includes what people do with an expression. An **epistemic goal** concerns what the practice seeks to establish or reveal. These are different analytical levels. A mathematical equation can be spoken, a poem can contain formal logic, an image can be evidential without being artistic, and a computer program can be simultaneously an executable procedure and a formal specification.

“Language” should therefore be read as a philosophical metaphor unless common requirements of syntax, semantics, and use have been demonstrated. Natural language has open-ended dialogue, convention, pragmatic context, and variable truth conditions. Mathematics involves notation, proof, calculation, axiomatization, diagrams, and structures that may have no direct sensory referent. The arts encompass literature, music, visual art, dance, performance, architecture, and conceptual practice. They do not share a single formal grammar.

Further, **knowledge expressed**, **knowledge created**, and **competence exercised** are distinct. One can accurately describe swimming while being unable to swim; an elegant equation can describe a false empirical model; an artwork can produce insight without making a factual assertion. If the hypothesis concerns all three, it must explain their relationships rather than assuming they are identical.

### 2.2. Working definitions of epistemic registers

| Register | Dominant affordances | Typical epistemic contribution | Characteristic risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Discursive (D)** | Narration, assertion, argument, instruction, contextual dialogue | Explanation, explicit testimony, coordination, negotiation of concepts | Ambiguity, rhetorical plausibility, missing assumptions |
| **Formal-relational (F)** | Explicit relations, quantification, transformations, algorithms, proof obligations | Deduction, prediction, verification, consistency checks | False premises, idealization, specification error |
| **Aesthetic-experiential (A)** | Expressive, imaginative, perceptual, temporal, spatial, and interpretive arrangement | Perspectival understanding, salience, exploration, embodied or interpretive insight | Non-unique interpretation, contextual dependence, weak factual warrant |

These are *ideal types*, not disjoint sets. An interactive data visualization may combine all three. A symphony may employ mathematical structure and cultural narrative. A legal argument may integrate formal rules with ordinary discourse. A register describes the **role an artifact plays in a task**, not its medium or institutional provenance.

We can propose a task-specific profile

$$
r(x,t) = (d,f,a), \qquad d,f,a \in [0,1],
$$

where $x$ is an artifact and $t$ a task. The coordinates would be obtained from independently specified coding rubrics; they need not sum to one. They are *candidate measurements*, not natural constants or factors already established by empirical data. Empirical work must establish inter-rater reliability, cultural and domain invariance, and predictive validity before interpreting these dimensions as real structure.

### 2.3. Why art is the difficult case

Art is not synonymous with nonverbal communication or sensory experience. A scientific photograph can be evidence without being art; literary art is made of ordinary words; conceptual art may make the role of perception secondary. The diversity of artistic practices makes a universal “language of art” particularly hard to delimit.

Nevertheless, some arts offer forms of understanding that are not conveniently reduced to propositions. Composition, dramatic perspective, rhythm, texture, ambiguity, and expressive choices can direct attention and enable interpretation. Their epistemic value is contested: aesthetic cognitivists defend knowledge-bearing roles for art, while critics question whether a powerful experience itself establishes transferable truths [3,12]. The reconstructed hypothesis therefore speaks of an **aesthetic-experiential register**, without claiming that every aesthetic judgment is factual knowledge or that every nonverbal datum is art.

## 3. Academic precedents and partial support

### 3.1. Cassirer: the plurality of symbolic forms

Ernst Cassirer understands human culture through symbolic forms that include language, myth, art, science, and other forms of meaning-making [2]. This is a major predecessor of any account that places artistic practices alongside language and formal science. Recent Cassirer scholarship explicitly examines art as a symbolic language [4].

Cassirer nonetheless challenges an exact threefold inventory. His forms are broader and do not map cleanly onto the proposed three. The warranted lesson is **symbolic plurality**, not proof that three forms exhaust cognition.

### 3.2. Goodman: notation, reference, and expression

Nelson Goodman's *Languages of Art* [3] studies denotation, exemplification, expression, notation, and the structure of symbolic systems. This yields a more useful analytical starting point than assuming that prose, equations, and art are parallel “languages.” Artistic and scientific artifacts can differ in how reference works and how symbol distinctions are made, not simply in whether they are heard or seen.

Goodman's analysis also blurs the proposed boundaries. Diagrams, musical scores, maps, verbal descriptions, and paintings can have overlapping symbolic functions. His work supports asking **what an artifact enables**, rather than sorting it exclusively by cultural genre.

### 3.3. Peirce: a competing triad

Peirce's icon, index, and symbol distinguish modes by which signs relate to objects [5]. An iconic diagram, an indexical measurement, and a conventional word can occur within a single mathematical paper or artwork. This threefold taxonomy is conceptually different from natural language, mathematics, and art. Its existence does not independently support the number three in the original hypothesis.

Peirce instead exposes the need to specify the **dimension being classified**. Sign-object relations, communicative systems, epistemic purposes, and academic disciplines are not interchangeable.

### 3.4. Bruner, Paivio, and diagrammatic reasoning

Bruner's enactive, iconic, and symbolic representation modes [6] recognize the importance of action and imagery alongside symbols. Paivio's dual-coding model [7] emphasizes relations between verbal and nonverbal representations. Neither directly endorses the proposed three languages; each is a serious rival.

Larkin and Simon [8] demonstrate why diagrams can sometimes make reasoning computationally easier than verbal descriptions by exposing relations and reducing search. This provides focused support for the broader claim that *representation format changes inferential affordances*. It does not establish a privileged artistic channel, because many diagrams are neither intended nor evaluated as artworks.

### 3.5. Mathematics and its own epistemology

The mathematical register requires more than a claim that equations are precise. Formal derivation preserves consequences *conditional on premises and rules*, while mathematical discovery, proof selection, modeling, and justification are diverse human practices. Kitcher's account of mathematical knowledge [9] is relevant because it places mathematical understanding in the history and practices of mathematicians, rather than reducing it to typographical formalism. Formal validity and empirical truth are separate matters: a rigorous model can still misdescribe the world.

### 3.6. Grounding, situated cognition, and linguistic form

Harnad's symbol-grounding problem [10], grounded cognition research [11], and Bender and Koller's distinction between linguistic form and meaning [13] raise problems for representational sufficiency. They do not establish that words alone convey no knowledge; text can transmit observations and discoveries made by others. They show instead that fluent manipulation of representations is not identical with successful reference, causal competence, or justified understanding.

These sources are **partial support and partial counterevidence**. They support multiple representational resources but challenge the leap from expression to universal ability.

## 4. Limits and counterexamples

### 4.1. The categories are not homogeneous

Natural languages are a family of symbol systems, mathematics is a cluster of formal practices, and art is a changing social and cultural category. Mathematics can be formulated in prose, literary art can be wholly linguistic, and musical form can be mathematically modeled. A taxonomy this broad can appear complete merely because artifacts may be assigned to whichever category is most convenient.

Overlapping categories are legitimate, but coverage obtained by **definition alone** is weak evidence. The hypothesis must be more predictive than merely saying that every knowledge practice uses words, structure, or experience.

### 4.2. Tacit, embodied, and procedural knowledge

A craftsperson's timing, an athlete's coordination, and a laboratory technician's skill can be instructed or described but are not thereby acquired. Such activities involve environmental constraints, feedback, practice, and context. We need not assert that the skills are literally unrepresentable to show that **representational mastery does not entail task performance**.

The strongest original conclusion [1], that command of the three languages permits any human activity, thus requires an independent theory of action, tools, goals, causal competence, and skill acquisition.

### 4.3. Art is not always a source of true propositions

Art can produce insight and perspective, but its interpretation may be plural and context-dependent. Some artworks assert or imply facts; others do not. Philosophical treatments of conceptual art [12] make this dispute especially visible. A research programme must distinguish **factive knowledge**, explanatory understanding, know-how, affective response, and aesthetic appreciation. No single accuracy metric appropriately scores all five.

Conversely, requiring art to behave like a theorem or an experiment would make the original decision to include it vacuous. We should permit heterogeneous epistemic outcomes while being explicit about which outcomes are truth-apt.

### 4.4. Why not two, four, or more?

An alternative might distinguish **verbal and nonverbal** representation [7], **enactive, iconic, and symbolic** modes [6], **icon, index, and symbol** [5], or unconstrained dimensions of representation. One could also propose diagrams, computation, gesture, measurement, or socially distributed coordination as additional categories.

Some are representational systems; others are evidence-generating or practical activities. A principled three-register model must specify membership and predictive consequences without simply absorbing every counterexample. If a two-factor or four-factor scheme better predicts performance, the present theory must be revised.

### 4.5. Mastery is not sufficiency

Even a machine able to write, prove, calculate, compose, and interpret at extraordinary levels could fail on a novel physical or social task. The inference from **expressive competence** to **universal performance** is invalid without further premises. Machine-generated knowledge and automated experimentation [14-16] are important technological developments, but their achievements must be evaluated by validated findings, calibrated uncertainty, and reproducibility rather than by text fluency.

## 5. A reconstructed hypothesis

We distinguish four claims.

**H1, prominence:** Discursive, formal-relational, and aesthetic-experiential orientations are recurrent and useful for describing practices in selected domains of knowledge creation and communication. This is a descriptive claim that requires sampling and coding across cultures and practices.

**H2, complementarity:** In specified tasks, appropriate combinations of registers improve relevant outcomes beyond any single register, when total information, computational resources, time, and training are matched. This does *not* predict that three always outperform two.

**H3, translation with constrained preservation:** Moving among registers can preserve some task-relevant properties while losing others. The preserved properties and assessment rules are defined *before* observing outcomes.

**H4, universal sufficiency:** Mastery of all three is sufficient for every distinct human activity. This is the ambitious original extrapolation and is **not supported by the literature reviewed here**. It cannot be evaluated until mastery, scope, and practical performance are independently operationalized.

Our proposed working definition is:

> **Three Languages Hypothesis, reconstructed:** Discursive, formal-relational, and aesthetic-experiential registers constitute three partially overlapping, task-relative orientations of human and machine representation. Their differing expressive and inferential constraints can create complementary contributions to knowledge-related tasks. Whether this threefold model is more explanatory or useful than alternative partitions remains an open empirical question.

This formulation is deliberately vulnerable to failure. The number three is not a premise that must be preserved at any cost.

### 5.1. Formalizing transformation without overstating it

Let $T_{ij}$ map artifacts from predominant register $i$ to register $j$ on task $t$. A proposed loss function is

$$
L_{ij}(x,t)=\sum_{p\in \mathcal{P}_t} w_p\,\ell_p\!\left(p(x),p(T_{ij}(x))\right),
$$

where $\mathcal{P}_t$ is a disclosed set of task-specific properties, $w_p$ their preregistered importance, and $\ell_p$ a property-sensitive discrepancy. For a mathematical proof, properties may include validity and the assumptions used. For a scientific plot, they may include quantitative relations and provenance. For an artwork, evaluation may involve interpretive fidelity, expressive purpose, and legitimate disagreement among qualified judges.

There is **no universal cross-domain semantic loss measure**, and the formula is a methodological proposal rather than a measured law. A transformation can improve accessibility while weakening formal guarantees; a proof checker can strengthen validity assurance while ignoring context. Claims about preservation require independent criteria.

## 6. Testable propositions

The following predictions concern defined tasks and rival hypotheses rather than a universal cognitive law.

**P1: Representational complementarity is task-dependent.** On tasks with difficult spatial or relational structures, matched diagrams or explicit formalizations will sometimes improve problem-solving performance beyond prose alone. Models must control information content and attention, not simply compare a richer stimulus against a poorer one [8].

**P2: Aesthetic contribution is not identical to visual contribution.** On independently selected interpretation or design tasks, aesthetic-experiential examples may improve expert-rated interpretation, originality, or compositional coherence after matched information and exposure. If the apparent effect is fully explained by novelty, presentation, or nonartistic diagrammatic utility, the distinct contribution attributed to A is weakened.

**P3: Translation exhibits testable preservation and loss.** Predefined invariants, such as logical entailments, numerical relationships, or agreed expressive constraints, will show different preservation rates under prose-to-formal, formal-to-diagram, and art-to-description transformations. This does not posit that every aesthetic property admits a numeric gold standard.

**P4: The model must beat rival taxonomies.** Independent coders should classify a diverse corpus of knowledge practices using D/F/A and rival two-, three-, four-, and continuous-feature models. Evaluate cross-domain stability, inter-rater agreement, held-out predictive performance, and complexity-adjusted fit. If the three-register model has no distinctive predictive value, it remains at most a useful teaching metaphor.

**P5: Expressive mastery and practical competence can dissociate.** Assess language, mathematics, and artistic output quality independently of practical performance on novel instrument-mediated, physical, or social tasks. If high three-register competence does not predict task success when tools and interventions matter, the corresponding restricted sufficiency claim fails. A reliable predictive relationship over defined domains would support a bounded version of H4, not unqualified universal sufficiency.

A concrete benchmark would contain matched task families emphasizing explanation, proof or constraint solving, aesthetic interpretation, and cross-register combinations. The same underlying model would receive different representations under fixed time, token, training, and tool budgets. Blind assessors and formal verifiers would score appropriate outcomes separately. Hold-out cases should include attractive but misleading diagrams, formally valid answers with false premises, and verbally convincing but non-executable procedures. Report error calibration, negative transfer, and cost, not only average success.

A corpus for P4 should include oral traditions, craft, legal practice, scientific instrumentation, industrial design, and non-Western arts. If taxonomy judgments depend heavily on educational background, that dependence is a finding about the framework rather than an error to conceal.

## 7. Quantitative and conceptual cautions

The proposed register coordinates are not empirically recovered latent variables. There is no demonstrated three-factor structure in human cognition. An instrument measuring the three registers may simply encode its designers' assumptions. Independent replication, counterexamples, and discriminant validity are essential.

Three categories may fit most examples because they are broad and overlap. **Coverage is not explanatory power.** To support a genuinely theoretical role, the taxonomy must improve prediction or understanding relative to reasonable alternatives, with a penalty for extra degrees of freedom.

Truth conditions also differ. The correctness of a proof, accuracy of a scientific assertion, and significance of an artistic interpretation are not interchangeable. Some aesthetic judgments are rationally contestable without being reducible to one canonical answer. Similarly, natural language is not intrinsically empirical, nor mathematical expression intrinsically true.

Claims about machine intelligence require separately validated measures. Token generation speed, document volume, and apparent fluency do not establish knowledge, artistic mastery, reliable mathematical reasoning, or autonomous causal discovery. The stronger extrapolations in the original proposal [1] should remain explicitly conditional.

## 8. Philosophical and technological relevance

**Philosophically**, the three-register model provides a structured question: under which conditions does understanding require explicit propositions, demonstrable relations, or experiential and interpretive organization? Cassirer and Goodman already motivate plural symbolic functions [2-4]. The value of the reconstruction is not to rediscover this plurality but to compare the usefulness of **these specific three** against rival schemes. Mathematical practice also illustrates why one must distinguish proof from empirical modeling and social knowledge production [9].

**Technologically**, an AI system could record *what role* a representation serves rather than classify artifacts merely as text, equation, or image. A translation component could emit explicit preservation obligations and uncertainty about discarded context. A theorem prover would verify formal consequences; an empirical subsystem would check observations; artistic judgments would use transparent criteria and multiple perspectives where appropriate.

A **representation router** could choose discourse, formal constraints, diagrams, aesthetic examples, or combinations according to task demands. Its merit would be measured against a strong baseline with the same computation and source information. Models that learn causal structure or reason with abstractions [17,19] offer motivation for this work, not confirmation that all intelligence decomposes into the three registers.

Methods from active learning and experimental design [20] suggest ways of choosing informative tests of proposed translations, while Kirsh and Maglio's analysis of epistemic action [18] reminds us that choosing a new representation is itself a cognitive intervention. These methods broaden the research programme without elevating action to a supposed fourth language.

## 9. Sensory stimulation as a side note

A rhythmic sound may be a musical composition, an auditory measurement, or an experimentally controlled stimulus. Its acoustic structure is not automatically artistic, and its physiological impact is not automatically knowledge. Sensory stimulation thus illustrates the distinction between **medium, epistemic register, and intervention**, rather than supplying an additional constitutive language.

Questions about neural entrainment, physiological benefits, or sensory interventions belong to a separate empirical programme. They are not evidence that the three-register hypothesis is true.

## 10. Conclusion

Natural language, mathematics, and art describe consequential and often complementary practices of understanding. Intellectual predecessors support plural ways of representing and interpreting the world, but they provide neither an exhaustive threefold partition nor grounds for equating mastery of expression with mastery of all activities.

The reconstructed hypothesis is best understood as a **comparative theory of representational affordances**. Its philosophical merit depends on clarifying different standards of understanding; its technological merit depends on whether task-sensitive representations and auditable transformations improve independently evaluated outcomes. The number three remains negotiable. A convincing account must survive the possibility that a different taxonomy works better.
